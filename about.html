Origin

A story about 24.7 megabytes

We are not a company. There is no funding, no office, no roadmap with dates. We are a group of people stopped by the same product limit, who decided to at least describe it accurately.

A Thursday in September 2026

At 19:59 a 24.7 MB quotation crawled through Outlook for two minutes, the progress bar reached 99%, and stopped. Four minutes later a message from postmaster arrived:

550 5.3.4 Message size exceeds fixed maximum message size

That night we learned three things. First, Outlook.com caps attachments at 20 MB. Second, that cap appears nowhere in the window where you write the message. Third, the official advice is “put the file in a shared location and send a link instead” — and the recipient was a procurement manager with a phone who does not open links.

Within the same hour we had a repository, a first splitting script, and a name taken from the error itself.

Timeline

The biography of one limit

We are not historians, but somebody was genuinely stuck in every one of these squares.

  • 1997
    The paperclip starts watching you type
    The Office 97 assistant becomes our acknowledged ancestor: it appeared at the worst possible moment and sincerely suggested you send a link instead.
  • 2003
    On-premises Exchange ships with 10 MB
    The default that haunted a generation of IT staff. It survived into the 2020s on countless internal servers because nobody dared touch it.
  • 2013
    Outlook.com draws the 20 MB line
    The same year OneDrive share links began appearing after every failure. Both halves of the story were now in place.
  • 2015
    Exchange Online moves to 25 MB
    The cheering lasted about a day, until somebody noticed the recipient was on Outlook.com.
  • 2026-09-13 · 19:59
    A 24.7 MB quotation is rejected
    Started at 19:59, society founded at 20:23, the same evening. This is the entire reason the site exists.
  • 2020
    Exchange Online defaults to 35 MB
    We wept with joy, then discovered the 20 MB Outlook.com limit was completely untouched. The world restored its usual absurdity.
  • 2023
    The limits database passes 40 services
    Every entry carries a reproduction method and a verification date, because “I think it was 25 MB” is not data.
  • 2026-09-13 · 20:23
    The society is actually founded
    Repository, site and six tools go up on the same day. Everything before this line is product history; from here on it is our own.
What we believe

Four rules unlikely to be overturned

Rule 01

Limits must be stated, not guessed

Every “attachment too large” should tell you the limit, who set it, and how to change it. A vague error is a failure of the software, not of the person reading it.

Rule 02

The recipient comes first

Our tools never require the recipient to install software, create an account, click a link, or upgrade a browser. If rebuilding a file is harder than a double-click, it was not delivered.

Rule 03

Never mock the person asking

There is exactly one exception: file naming. Any filename containing “final”, “final2” or “FINAL-for-real” will be politely offered att split --limit 20MB.

Rule 04

Everything must be reproducible

Data needs a method, conclusions need a capture, bugs need a minimal reproduction. “It doesn't work here” is not a report. “It doesn't work here, and here are 12 lines” is.

Governance

How we argue, and how we stop

Anything that affects users goes through an RFC: public proposal, two weeks of discussion, maintainer review. Votes count heads, not commits — writing more does not mean being right.

  • Cross-project decisions are settled by a rotating group of five maintainers on six-month terms.
  • Any deadlock older than two weeks is closed by a coin toss. The coin is a 20 MB commemorative.
  • Reversed decisions stay in the repository with the reason they failed. We do not delete history.
  • No lifetime positions. Two quiet quarters and you become an honorary member.
A note on the name We are called attachmenttoolarge because that is what Outlook said, not because we like the situation. The name is a souvenir, not a goal.
