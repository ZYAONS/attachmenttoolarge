We built a monument to 20 MB

The inscription is six bytes. One of them is a newline — because the contributor who carved it insisted that a monument should end with a newline, the way a letter should end with a signature.

At a community meeting last winter, someone suggested that we ought to do something about 20 MB. The proposal sat in the chat log in silence for forty seconds — in an open-source project, forty seconds of silence is close to unanimous consent.

Why 20 MB

Because it is the wall most of us hit first. Not 35 MB, not 10 MB, but the 20 MB at Outlook.com that appears nowhere in the compose window and only shows itself after you press Send. It has its own error, its own error code, and its own gentle piece of advice:

Try putting the file in a shared location and sending a link instead.

We have read that sentence so many times that it has taken on the character of a prayer: polite, logically sound, and no help at all with your actual problem.

The inscription

The final inscription is below, six bytes in total:

20 MB

Yes, the trailing newline counts as a byte. Some objected that a newline is not part of an inscription and should be counted as five bytes. The proposer replied: “The inscription is six bytes because six is divisible by two three times. We do not have coincidences in this society.” The vote was 23 to 4.

What we argued about on unveiling day

  • Font. Some argued for monospace, on the grounds that “this is a monument for engineers”; others held that monospace looks like a log file, not a monument. The compromise: monospace for the inscription, sans-serif for the plinth.
  • Whether to put a date on it. A date would let people assume 20 MB is over. We all know it is not — it is still stopping people today. The date on the plinth reads still maintained.
  • Whether to carve the error code. The proposal was to cut 0x80040610 into the back, the way the back of an epitaph works: visible only to those willing to walk around. Passed unanimously.

What a monument can do

Honestly, nothing. It will not raise the limit by a single byte, and it will not save anyone four minutes. But it turns something long treated as “you failed to configure it properly” into a public object — from here on, you are not the only one stuck at 99%.

That is the entire idea behind this site: collect the vague errors, translate them into plain language, stamp them with a date and a source, and leave them somewhere other people can find them. The rest is for the tools. What the tools cannot fix, we complain about together.

If you want one of your own

The source of the monument lives in the /monument directory of the repository: under two hundred lines of HTML. You can carve your own number into it — 10 MB, 25 MB, 50 MB, or that gateway at your company nobody dares to touch.

One rule: keep the trailing newline in the inscription. It is the least useful, and the least dispensable, of the six bytes on the stone.
